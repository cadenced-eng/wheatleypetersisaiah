## Critical Introduction to "Isaiah lxiii. 1-8"
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A paraphrase of the Old Testament for which it is named, Phillis Wheatley Peters’s poem "Isaiah lxiii. 1-8" retells the prophecy detailed in Isaiah's sixty-third chapter, making poetic comparisons to the “Almighty Savior['s]” work in a wine press and a righteous vengeance he enacts across a battlefield (Wheatley Peters line 9). Wheatley Peters's musings on the Christian faith are the focus of some of her most well known and, occasionally, controversial poems, such as “On being brought from Africa to America,” also included in Wheatley Peters's Poems on Various Subjects, Religious and Moral. The poem has been the subject of much criticism by poets and scholars alike (Jeffers 90, Thorn 121), but has also been praised for the complicated rhetorical work that Wheatley Peters accomplishes in a few lines. To better understand the way the author utilized biblical allusions in "Isaiah lxiii. 1-8," we can look to a reading of this poem by William J. Scheick, which illuminates Wheatley Peters's messaging, as well as the implications it has on her other biblically centred work.
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Scheick identifies that the ambiguity created by the use of punctuation in the final lines of “On being brought from Africa to America” “refutes the notion, held by many of Wheatley's contemporaries, that Cain, marked by God, is the progenitor of the black race only…. [instating] both Christians and [Black Americans] as the mutual offspring of Cain who are subject to refinement by divine grace“ establishing an equality between humanity in their Christian faith (Scheick 136). That this aligns with our understanding of her politics from the broader collection of both published and unpublished poetry, as well as letters and newspaper publications, gives credence to this reading of the poem (Carretta 176). For those looking to better understand "Isaiah lxiii. 1-8," this reading establishes Wheatley Peters's use of familiar biblical imagery and theology to argue her own politics and values, specifically of an innate equality of Black and white Christians. Understanding this reading of Wheatley Peter's messaging present in the fifth poem of Wheatley Peters’s collection creates a framework for interpreting the significantly more metaphorically veiled "Isaiah lxiii. 1-8" tucked further into the collection. 
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Readers of “Isaiah lxiii. 1-8” will likely quickly identify the references to the atonement of Christ, the “king or mighty God” whose “soul [he] sacrific'd with willing hand” so mankind could be redeemed (Wheatley Peters lines 1, 16). What defines the poem's specific depiction of Christ's sacrifice is the emphasis on isolation, the performance of labour ‘forsaken’ by all but himself, and the physical cost of this labour, which all point towards a Christ figure coded in the same language that evokes the experience of enslavement (lines 7-11). The wine-press becomes pivotal in communicating this imagery. In comparison to its source material, “Isaiah lxiii. 1-8” obscures the gore found in these verses by focusing its attention on the symbolic wine itself. In the King James Version translation of The Bible, it is stated explicitly that the dye staining the subject's “apparel” is the blood of the enemies of the Lord (King James Version, Isaiah 63.2-3). On the other hand, while not lacking violence, Wheatley Peters’s poem depicts “his purple vesture” as stained by the wine that “bled, and pour'd the gushing purple round” (Wheatley Peters line 8). The symbolic connection between wine and blood would be a known comparison for Wheatley Peters' Christian audience, familiar with the ritual of communion. What the change accomplishes is bringing the reader's attention to the cost of the physical labour intrinsically tied to production of the wine, a commodity. Using personifying language that describes the press as ‘bleeding’ and ‘groaning’ depicts Christ as bearing these physical costs of this production just as he bore the sins of the world (lines 7-8). 
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This association that Wheatley Peters develops has further theological implications. Take for example the following couplet from the second stanza: “‘For man's release [the Saviour] sustain'd the pond'rous load,/’For man the wrath of an immortal God’” (line 13-14). In the context of the labour imagery, the term ‘release’ holds a double meaning, connecting the release of humanity from sin and release from enslavement, intertwining Christ's sacrificial atonement (the keystone of Christian theology) with an argument about the morality of slavery. Following this, a reader can make assumptions about who is numbered among the “haughty foes” receiving his “vengeance” (line 22). 
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Just as Wheatley Peters demonstrated in “On being brought from Africa to America,” the poet adeptly draws on well-known and recognizable Christian imagery in "Isaiah lxiii. 1-8" and reframes it, arguing the incongruency of slavery within Christian practice and imbuing a depiction of the abolitionist cause with the energy and imperative of a righteous, holy battle.

## ISAIAH LXIII. 1-8.
<br>SAY, heav’nly muse, what king or mighty God, 
<br>  That moves sublime from Idumea’s road? {% include essay/feature/aside.html text="annotation." %}
<br>  In Bosrah’s dies, with martial glories join’d,
<br>  His purple vesture waves upon the wind.
  Why thus enrob’d delights he to appear
  In the dread image of the Pow’r of war?
    Compres’d in wrath the swelling wine-press{% include essay/feature/aside.html
   text="annotation." %} groan’d,
  It bled, and pour’d the gushing purple round.
    “Mine was the act,” th’ Almighty Saviour said,
  And shook the dazzling glories of his head,
  “When all forsook I trod the press alone,
  “And conquer’d by omnipotence my own;
  “For man’s release sustain’d the pond’rous load,
  “For man the wrath of an immortal God:
  “To execute th’ Eternal’s dread command
  “My soul I sacrific’d with willing hand;
  “Sinless I stood before the avenging frown,
  “Atoning thus for vices not my own.”
     His eye the ample field of battle round
  Survey’d, but no created succours found;
  His own omnipotence sustain’d the right,
  His vengeance sunk the haughty foes in night;
  Beneath his feet the prostrate troops were spread,
  And round him lay the dying, and the dead.
    Great God, what light’ning flashes from thine eyes?
  What pow’r withstands if thou indignant rise?
    Against thy Zion though her foes may rage,
  And all their cunning, all their strength engage,
  Yet she serenely on thy bosom lies,
  Smiles at their arts, and all their force defies.
